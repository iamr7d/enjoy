Slide 1 — Title (0:00–0:20)

JOY: Artificial Viewer From gaze prediction to closed-loop computational observation MRes Creative Computing, UAL

Say: This project asks what it takes to build something that watches a film, rather than something that predicts where a person watching a film will look. Those turn out to be different problems, and the difference is measurable.

Slide 2 — Two problems that look like one (0:20–1:40)

Left: a model is handed a frame a person looked at, and scores how much probability it put where they looked. Right: a system generates its own fixation, and then sees the world from wherever that fixation left it.

Say: The standard way to evaluate an attention model is prediction. You give it a frame that a person actually looked at, it produces a probability map, and you score it against the real fixation. Even the best scanpath evaluations work this way: they score each fixation given the human's previous scanpath. At every step, the model is handed back the human's trajectory.

An observer does not get that. It has to choose a fixation, live with the choice, and perceive the scene from wherever it ended up. If it chooses badly, the next percept is degraded, and the error compounds. A predictor's errors are independent. An observer's errors accumulate.

The central claim of this thesis is that the distinction is not philosophical. I can show you a policy that reliably improves prediction and fails to improve observation, in the same data, with the same weights.

Slide 3 — Research questions (1:40–2:20)

RQ1 What does it take, and what constrains it? RQ2 What do semantic, bottom-up and oculomotor guidance contribute, and is that state-dependent? RQ3 What do limited access and temporal history change? RQ4 Can narrative state help without omniscience?

Say: Four questions. What does it actually take to build an observer that behaves like a human, and which components constrain that. What each source of guidance contributes, and whether that contribution depends on the viewer's state. What limited perception and history change. And whether narrative understanding can contribute without giving the system information no viewer had.

Each is answerable from the experiments, including where the answer is negative, and several are.

Slide 4 — Context (2:20–3:40)

Central bias survives control of motor and image factors — Tatler 2007 Gaze in dynamic scenes clusters on motion — Mital et al. 2011 Fixation duration has its own timing model — Nuthmann et al. 2010 Meaning versus salience is unresolved — Henderson & Hayes 2017; Pedziwiatr et al. 2021 Comprehension changes expectation, not location — SPECT, Loschky et al. 2020

Say: Five things from the literature frame the problem.

First, most of gaze is not about meaning. The central bias is large and survives controls for both motor and image factors. In dynamic scenes, viewers cluster tightly on the same locations, and that clustering is predicted by motion. So a semantic cue is never competing in a vacuum.

Second, where to look and how long to look are separate problems with separate models.

Third, whether semantic maps really capture meaning is genuinely unresolved. Meaning maps were shown not to outperform a saliency model built on semantically neutral high-level features. Any project that adds a semantic channel and reports an improvement inherits that ambiguity, including this one, and I say so in the thesis.

Fourth, the theory that covers narrative viewing separates what happens within a fixation from event models built across fixations, and holds that comprehension does not tell you where to look. It changes what you expect. That is a design constraint I adopted rather than an assumption.

Slide 5 — The Artificial Viewer (3:40–5:40) · Figure 1

frame → foveate at own fixation → percept percept → saliency map M, semantic map V plus history G from its own past fixations, plus centre bias C P = a_M·M + a_G·G + a_C·C + a_V·V sample fixation → draw duration → advance clock → repeat

Say: Here is the loop. The frame is foveated at the viewer's own current fixation, giving a percept that is sharp in the middle and degraded towards the edge. Both evidence models run on that percept, not on the frame. That matters: a vision-language model asked about a full frame is an omniscient observer, with detail no retina delivers. If you do not constrain its input, you have quietly given your viewer perfect peripheral vision, and any resemblance to human behaviour becomes uninterpretable.

Two more sources join them. A history term built from the viewer's own previous fixations, not from any human's. And a centre bias.

The four combine as a weighted mixture on the simplex. I chose a mixture rather than a fused network for one reason: it keeps every cue's contribution identifiable as a single number, which is what makes the whole thesis measurable. Then a fixation is sampled, a duration is drawn, the clock advances, and the loop runs again from the new percept.

No human fixation enters at inference time, and every generated record carries the source of its history so that claim can be checked rather than trusted.

Slide 6 — Materials and protocol (5:40–7:00)

75 clips · 73 videos · 4,492 frames · 223 observers · 4.6M fixation samples 45 validation tests, all passing folds at video level · paired bootstrap · per-fold agreement · inner dev split

Say: The film data is DIEM: 75 clips, 4,492 sampled frames, 223 observers, four and a half million identified fixation samples. Every frame has at least eight observers.

The dataset is checked by 45 named tests covering schema, keys, geometry, normalisation, leakage and reproducibility. Two real defects were caught by those tests rather than by inspection: clips whose variants had landed in different folds, and semantic regions extending outside the frame.

On protocol, three commitments, and I will come back to why they matter. Folds are formed at video level so variants cannot straddle them. Comparisons are paired and bootstrapped, and every pooled interval is reported with how many folds agree in sign. And where a model needs epoch selection, that happens on an inner development split, never on the evaluation set.

Slide 7 — Experiment 1: the semantic channel (7:00–8:20) · Figure 3b

per-frame fitted semantic weight: 0.0475 single global weight fitted for held-out likelihood: 0.000 in four of five folds saliency 0.69 · history 0.30 oracle headroom over fixed weights: 0.2108 bits

Say: First result, and it is the one I would lead with.

Fit the mixture weights per frame and the semantic channel gets about 0.05. Fit a single global weight set to maximise held-out likelihood, and the semantic weight is zero in four of five folds.

Those are consistent, and the reconciliation is the finding. A per-frame fit can put mass on the semantic map for the minority of frames where it helps. One global weight cannot, so it puts none. The semantic channel is real and local, not absent, and a model forced to commit to one weight does better ignoring it.

Saliency and history take 0.69 and 0.30 between them. And the entire gap between fixed weights and a per-frame oracle is 0.21 bits. Hold that number: every semantic and narrative effect in this thesis is a fraction of it.

Slide 8 — Experiment 2: two senses of state dependence (8:20–9:30) · Figure 5

predicting the fitted weight: ρ 0.44, ceiling ≈ 0.81 — works using the prediction to guide gaze: +0.0094 bits, 5 of 5 folds — reliable, 5% of headroom eight-feature policy: worse than fixed on three of five folds

Say: If the semantic weight varies frame to frame, it should vary with something observable. It does. State features predict the fitted weight at a correlation of 0.44, against a reliability ceiling of about 0.81, so they explain a little under half of the reliable variation. That is a real finding about when semantic guidance matters.

Now use that prediction to actually weight the gaze model. The gain is 0.0094 bits. Reliable, five folds out of five, and about five per cent of the available headroom.

And give the policy more freedom, eight features instead of five, and it becomes worse than fixed weights on three of five folds.

So predicting a fitted latent parameter is not the same as improving the behaviour that parameter was fitted to describe. That is the clearest methodological lesson in the project.

Slide 9 — The dissociation (9:30–11:00) · Figure 3, centrepiece

Same policy, two evaluations frame level, 4,088 frames: +0.0099 bits, positive in 5 of 5 folds closed loop, 8 clips: mean +0.391, median −0.919, wins 3 of 8 clips prediction is evaluated on the human trajectory; observation must survive its own

Say: This is the centre of the thesis.

Take the best arbitration policy. Evaluated as a predictor, on frames the audience actually fixated, it beats fixed weights by 0.0099 bits, positive in all five folds. Reliable.

Now put the same policy in charge of the loop, generating its own scanpath and updating its own state. Its mean advantage is positive, at 0.391, but its median is minus 0.919, and it wins on three of eight clips. The mean rests on two clips. When mean and median disagree in sign, the average is being carried by outliers.

So a model got better at predicting where people looked, and did not get better at looking. The mechanism is the one on the slide. A predictor is scored on the human trajectory and is never asked to survive its own errors. An observer is.

One honest caveat, which is in the thesis. The two evaluations differ in power as well as in kind: 4,088 frames and 75 clips against 8 clips. The dissociation is also consistent with a real but small benefit that the closed-loop design is too weak to detect. What cannot be said is that the closed loop confirms the frame-level result.

Slide 10 — Experiment 5 and the behavioural gap (11:00–12:20) · Figures 8, 10

story state: +0.0041 bits · shuffle its time: −0.0153 · take it from another clip: −0.0214 load-bearing part is event boundary; object identity contributes nothing detectable humans 179 ms · 1.44° · 46% return viewer 214–220 ms · 2.4–4.7° · 4–24% return

Say: Briefly on narrative, then the finding that matters most.

A story state, computed only from the viewer's own prior outputs and audited to contain no future information, adds 0.0041 bits. Small. But shuffle its timing and you lose three times that; take it from a different clip and you lose five times that. Both shuffles fall below having no story state at all, in all five folds. A signal that loses more when disturbed than it gains by being present is doing temporal work. And the load-bearing part is event boundary structure. Object identity contributes nothing detectable.

Now the behavioural comparison. The audience made fixations of 179 milliseconds, saccades of 1.44 degrees, and returned to a recently fixated spot 46 per cent of the time. Every rule I tested produced fixations of 214 to 220 milliseconds, saccades of 2.4 to 4.7 degrees, and return rates of 4 to 24 per cent.

That gap is in timing, amplitude and persistence. No semantic or narrative mechanism in this thesis touches any of them, and all of them are far larger than every effect I have shown you.

Slide 11 — Contribution and limitations (12:20–13:20)

  1. an empirical dissociation between prediction and observation
  2. semantic guidance quantified: real, local, zero under a global weight
  3. a causally audited narrative state that never names a location
  4. three protocol failures, each of which reversed a result here limits: 8 clips underpowered · video saliency model on stills · observers not held out · baseline not comparable to published information gain

Say: Four contributions.

The dissociation. A quantified account of the semantic channel, including that a global weight sets it to zero. A narrative state that is causally audited and influences gaze only through arbitration, never by naming a location. And a methodological one: three protocol decisions each reversed a result in this project. Selecting the epoch on the evaluation set moved the same comparison from minus 0.094 to plus 0.103, nearly two tenths of a bit, which is further than the effect being measured. A pooled interval without fold agreement supported a claim living entirely in one fold. A mean over eight clips without the median made the leading rule one that loses on most of them. And a training-size sweep showed the state-dependent rung loses to fixed below about three thousand examples and beats it above, monotonically, so a negative measured below that size is a statement about the training set and not the mechanism.

The first three inflate the more flexible model. The fourth deflates it. Between them, an evaluation of adaptive against fixed can be made to come out either way without touching the mechanism at all.

Limitations. The closed loop uses eight clips and is underpowered. The bottom-up model is a video model, and one study applies it to stills where its motion pathway is inert. Observers are not held out. And my metric is bits above uniform, not the benchmark convention of information gain above a centre bias, so my numbers are not directly comparable with published ones.

Slide 12 — Conclusion (13:20–13:40)

Human-like artificial viewing is not reducible to better semantic gaze prediction. The binding constraints are perceptual access, temporal continuity and motor dynamics.

Say: I did not build a human-like observer. What I built was an instrument that shows where the attempt succeeds and where it fails, and the failures are informative. Each time I added semantic or narrative sophistication it returned less than the last, while the largest gap sat somewhere none of it touched.

So: human-like artificial viewing is not reducible to better semantic gaze prediction. The binding constraints are perceptual access, temporal continuity and motor dynamics. That is what the next programme addresses.

Thank you.

Anticipated questions

Are you saying adaptive arbitration is impossible? No. The experiments show that the particular state-dependent formulations evaluated here provide limited benefit at low capacity, and that this benefit does not survive increased model freedom, autonomous closed-loop deployment, or transfer to task-directed search. The results constrain the hypothesis rather than falsify the general possibility.

Isn't 0.004 bits too small to mean anything? It is statistically resolved and behaviourally negligible, and the thesis reports those as separate things. The point of quoting the 0.21 bit oracle headroom is to make the scale visible.

Why is the semantic weight zero in one place and 0.08 in another? They are different estimators. Per-frame fits, a single global fit, and an earlier population fit on eight held-out clips give 0.0475, 0.000 and 0.084 respectively. The thesis names the estimator every time it quotes a number, and the disagreement between them is a result rather than an inconsistency.

Could the semantic map just be high-level image structure? Possibly, and that is unresolved in the literature too. The test that would settle it is substituting a high-level saliency model for the semantic map and refitting. It is scoped and not run, and it is an early component of the proposed programme.
